# WHI-001-CP002 — Source and Coverage Plan

**Checkpoint:** Renaissance, Reformation, Science, and Exploration  
**Parent:** WHI-001 World History  
**Status:** Content build in progress; English human review precedes localization and Question Studio registration.  
**Audience:** SSC, Banking, Railways, Defence, and state-level objective examinations; PSC/UPSC extension where the facts support comparison.

## Scope and ownership

This checkpoint covers the Renaissance and humanism; movable-type printing; major artists, writers and patrons; the Protestant Reformation; the Scientific Revolution; European maritime routes; early overseas expansion; and the Atlantic system. It does not repeat CP001’s ancient and medieval survey. Enlightenment and Atlantic revolutions belong to CP003. The Industrial Revolution and its imperial links belong to CP005 and CP007.

## Planned 60-question review pool

- Easy: 18; Medium: 30; Hard: 12.
- At least 10 question families, including direct recall, person–work/discovery, movement–idea, chronology, cause and consequence, document–outcome, matched pairs, incorrect relationship, route identification, and multi-statement selection.
- Four options per item; answer positions checked after content review and randomized only in production.
- Every question must have a fact ID, source ID, difficulty, family, and a specific explanation. Exact years are used only where sources support them.

## Canonical coverage inventory

### Renaissance, humanism, and print

1. Renaissance centres in Italy, with Florence as a major artistic and humanist centre.
2. Humanist study of classical Greek and Roman writing.
3. Humanist interest in education, rhetoric, history, and human capacities.
4. Civic and family patronage as a source of commissions and support.
5. Medici support for artists and learning in Florence.
6. Gutenberg’s movable metal type in western European book production.
7. Gutenberg Bible printed in Mainz around the mid-fifteenth century; describe it as the first major western European book using movable metal type, not the first printed book worldwide.
8. Printing’s role in reproducing and circulating texts without claiming that manuscripts disappeared.
9. Petrarch’s role in the recovery and study of classical writing.
10. Dante and the literary importance of vernacular Italian.

### Artists and writers

11. Leonardo da Vinci and *The Last Supper*.
12. Leonardo da Vinci and the *Mona Lisa*.
13. Michelangelo and the Sistine Chapel ceiling.
14. Michelangelo and the sculpture *David*.
15. Raphael and *The School of Athens*.
16. Erasmus and *In Praise of Folly*.
17. Thomas More and *Utopia*.
18. Niccolò Machiavelli and *The Prince*.
19. The relationship between Renaissance artistic production and patronage.
20. Humanist writing as a varied body of work, not a single political or religious doctrine.

### Reformation

21. Martin Luther and the Ninety-five Theses, dated 1517.
22. Luther’s criticism of indulgence practices.
23. Wittenberg as the setting associated with Luther’s early teaching and the theses.
24. Printing’s contribution to circulation of Reformation arguments.
25. Luther’s appearance before the Diet of Worms in 1521 and refusal to recant.
26. The Edict of Worms and condemnation of Luther’s writings.
27. John Calvin and Geneva’s place in Reformed Protestantism.
28. Henry VIII and England’s break with papal authority.
29. The Act of Supremacy (1534) and royal supremacy over the Church of England.
30. Council of Trent (1545–1563) and Catholic reform; do not conflate it with Protestant reform.

### Scientific Revolution

31. Copernicus and the heliocentric model.
32. *De revolutionibus orbium coelestium* published in 1543.
33. Galileo’s telescopic observations and the changing study of the heavens.
34. Galileo’s observations of Jupiter’s moons as evidence that not every celestial body orbited Earth.
35. Galileo’s 1633 trial and formal recantation, with careful wording about the historical context.
36. Kepler’s laws and elliptical planetary orbits.
37. Newton’s *Principia Mathematica* (1687).
38. Newton’s laws of motion and universal gravitation.
39. Vesalius and observation-based human anatomy.
40. Vesalius’s *On the Fabric of the Human Body* (1543).
41. William Harvey and the circulation of blood.
42. Harvey’s *De motu cordis* (1628).
43. Francis Bacon and the promotion of systematic empirical investigation.
44. Scientific claims tested through observation, measurement, and mathematical reasoning.
45. Distinguish Copernicus, Galileo, Kepler, Newton, Vesalius, Harvey, and Bacon by contribution.

### Maritime exploration and Atlantic system

46. Portuguese voyages along the west coast of Africa and the search for sea routes to Asian trade.
47. Bartolomeu Dias rounding Africa’s southern cape in 1488.
48. Vasco da Gama’s voyage around Africa and arrival at Calicut in 1498.
49. Christopher Columbus’s westward Atlantic voyage in 1492, seeking an Asian route; avoid describing the Americas as uninhabited or “discovered” without qualification.
50. Columbus’s arrival in the Caribbean and his mistaken belief that he had reached the Indies.
51. Treaty of Tordesillas (1494), agreed by Spain and Portugal, and its line of demarcation for overseas claims.
52. Pedro Álvares Cabral’s arrival on the coast of Brazil in 1500.
53. Magellan’s expedition beginning in 1519 under Spanish sponsorship.
54. Magellan’s death in the Philippines in 1521.
55. Juan Sebastián Elcano’s role in bringing the surviving expedition back to Spain in 1522.
56. The expedition’s completion of the first circumnavigation; avoid attributing the completed return voyage personally to Magellan.
57. Overseas voyages linking European ports with routes and settlements across the Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific oceans.
58. Trade, conquest, settlement, and migration as parts of the Atlantic system.
59. Forced transportation of Africans through the transatlantic slave trade; describe the human violence and coercion accurately.
60. Unequal power and outcomes within Atlantic exchange; do not imply that connected communities shared equal gains.
