## CP002 · ENG008-RC2-E09 — A City App Should Not Become the Only Door [editorial] — 252 words

City governments increasingly use mobile apps for complaints, bill payments, service requests and public notices. For many residents, this is genuinely convenient. A photograph of a broken streetlight can be uploaded in seconds, and a digital receipt makes it easier to track whether a request has been registered.

The problem begins when convenience quietly turns into exclusivity. Not every resident owns a capable smartphone, keeps mobile data active or feels comfortable completing official forms on a small screen. Older people, temporary workers and residents sharing devices may face barriers that are invisible to designers who assume constant connectivity.

Keeping an offline option does not require rejecting digital services. It simply recognises that a public service has a different responsibility from a private app. A company may choose the customers it serves; a civic system is expected to remain usable by the public it represents.

The better standard is therefore not “digital first” or “paper first,” but “accessible by more than one practical route.” Apps can remain the fastest channel while phone, desk or assisted options protect people who cannot use them. Efficiency matters, but a service is not fully efficient if it saves staff time by shifting unreasonable difficulty onto citizens.

The same principle applies when systems are redesigned. If an app becomes easier for regular users but removes the only practical channel used by a smaller group, the overall service may look more streamlined while becoming less accessible. Good digital design therefore measures who gains convenience and who may lose access.

## CP004 · ENG008-BM-B02 — When a Discount Makes a Subscription Harder to Judge [business] — 462 words

Subscription businesses often advertise savings by comparing a monthly plan with a discounted annual payment. The annual figure may genuinely be cheaper, but the comparison can still be difficult for customers because price is only one part of the commitment. A lower monthly equivalent may require a large payment in advance, limit cancellation or make unused months more costly.

This creates a familiar decision problem. Consumers naturally focus on the visible discount, while the less visible question is whether they will actually use the service long enough for the discount to matter. A gym membership, software plan or learning platform can be inexpensive per month on paper and still be poor value for someone whose needs change after a few weeks.

Businesses sometimes defend long plans by arguing that predictable revenue allows them to offer lower prices. That can be true. Stable subscriptions can reduce billing costs and make planning easier. The concern is not that annual plans are inherently unfair, but that headline savings can dominate attention even when the commitment terms are equally important.

Cancellation design matters here. If a customer can clearly see renewal dates, pause rules and refund conditions before paying, the annual plan remains a choice that can be compared with alternatives. If those terms are hidden behind several screens or described vaguely, the same discount becomes harder to evaluate because the true cost of changing one's mind is unclear.

There is also a behavioural effect. Once customers have paid in advance, they may continue using a service simply because they have already spent the money, even when another option would suit them better. This does not prove that annual plans reduce welfare; it shows that payment structure can influence later decisions as well as the initial purchase.

For regulators and product designers, the useful question is therefore not whether discounts should exist. It is whether the comparison gives customers enough information to understand both savings and commitment. A percentage-off label is simple, but simplicity can mislead when important conditions are pushed outside the main decision.

A better interface might show the total amount due, the equivalent monthly cost, the renewal date and the cancellation consequence in the same place. Such disclosure would not force customers toward monthly or annual payment. It would make the trade-off more visible before they commit.

The broader lesson extends beyond subscriptions. Price presentation shapes attention. A number can be accurate and still give an incomplete picture if it highlights one advantage while leaving the relevant cost of flexibility in the background. Good comparison tools should therefore make important trade-offs easier to see, not merely make the cheapest-looking number larger on the screen. Even a short disclosure can improve comparison if it appears at the moment of choice rather than after payment.

## CP004 · ENG008-BM-H02 — Why Average Waiting Time Can Hide the Hardest Cases [health] — 450 words

Hospitals often report average waiting time because it compresses thousands of patient experiences into one understandable number. If the average falls from forty minutes to thirty, the change appears straightforward. Yet an average can improve while a smaller group of patients continues to wait much longer than everyone else.

Imagine a clinic where most patients are seen within twenty minutes but a few complex cases wait two hours because they require a specialist room. If scheduling changes make the quick visits even faster, the overall average may fall although the longest waits barely change. The headline measure would show progress, but not where the remaining problem is concentrated.

This does not make averages useless. Managers need broad indicators to compare periods, departments and interventions. The difficulty arises when one number is treated as if it describes the shape of the entire distribution. Median waiting time, the share of patients seen within a target, and the longest typical waits can reveal different aspects of the same system.

Patient experience also depends on uncertainty. A forty-minute wait may feel manageable when a patient is told what to expect, while an unexplained twenty-minute delay can be stressful when someone fears they have been forgotten. Communication cannot replace timely care, but it can change how predictable the process feels.

There is another complication: not all waiting has the same cause. Delays may come from late arrivals, emergency cases, room shortages, missing records or a mismatch between appointment length and patient need. Reducing one cause can improve the average while leaving another group untouched.

For this reason, quality teams increasingly examine waiting time by patient type, time of day and stage of the visit. They may separate registration delay from time waiting for a clinician or test. Such breakdowns make the data less tidy but more useful for deciding where a process should change.

The same principle applies when evaluating reforms. A new booking system should not be judged only by whether the average falls. Reviewers should ask whether extreme waits decline, whether access becomes more consistent across patient groups and whether improvement in one stage simply moves delay to another stage.

The wider lesson is about measurement rather than hospitals alone. Summary statistics help us see patterns, but they also hide variation by design. Good management therefore uses simple indicators as signals to investigate, not as substitutes for understanding who is still experiencing the problem and why. The same caution applies to comparisons between hospitals: one facility may treat a very different mix of patients, so a lower average cannot automatically be read as better performance. Measures become more meaningful when the populations, stages and definitions being compared are made fully explicit in advance.
