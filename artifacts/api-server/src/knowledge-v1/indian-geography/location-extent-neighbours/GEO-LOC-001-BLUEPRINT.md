## 1. Goal

Build an exam-standard, source-governed chapter covering India's astronomical location, size and extent, reference lines, standard time, extreme points, surrounding seas, land and maritime neighbours, island location, border/coastal map relations and mixed map-based reasoning.

Learner-facing language must stay concise, natural and suitable for SSC, Banking, Railway and comparable State exams. Facts should be tested through direct recall, reverse association, coordinate reasoning, direction and bounded statement questions rather than repetitive template wording.

## 2. Source policy

Primary educational authorities:
- NCERT `Contemporary India-I`, Chapter 1 — India: Size and Location;
- NIOS Geography (316), Lesson 16 — India: Physical Features.

Supporting Government of India sources may be used where a standard textbook fact needs precise administrative or cartographic framing.

Runtime web or LLM output is never truth authority. Changing political claims, live boundary disputes, current administrative proposals and unstable statistics are outside the permanent static pool unless separately versioned.

## 4. Chapter spine

1. Northern and Eastern Hemisphere position
2. Mainland latitude and longitude limits
3. Tropic of Cancer
4. Standard Meridian of India
5. Mainland dimensions and total area
6. East–west local-time difference and standard time
7. Extreme points and mainland/island distinction
8. Arabian Sea, Bay of Bengal and Indian Ocean
9. Land neighbours and directional relations
10. Sri Lanka, Maldives, straits and channels
11. Andaman–Nicobar and Lakshadweep location
12. State/UT map associations, coastal/border geography and mixed coordinate reasoning

## 5. Question-quality rules

- Stems must read like real competitive-exam questions.
- Avoid mechanical wording such as `associated with`, `best describes`, `in the context of`, `broad/broadly` and unnecessary `mainly`.
- Every permanent QL owns six semantically distinct questions where the source supports them.
- Explanations should usually use 2–3 short sentences: answer first, then one useful geographic connection.
- Distractors must remain plausible geographic coordinates, lines, distances, directions, countries, seas, states/UTs or map relations.
- Hard questions should require comparison, elimination, coordinate reasoning or multi-fact interpretation rather than difficult English.
- Do not turn disputed boundary claims into learner-facing certainty. Use stable textbook framing and keep the permanent pool static.
- No option-by-option analysis unless genuinely needed.

## 6. Difficulty model

- Easy: direct identification, standard figure or one clear map relation.
- Medium: reverse association, coordinate elimination, comparison or two-clue reasoning.
- Hard: multi-fact coordinate/time/location reasoning or statement integration.
